1. Why College Students Need Financial Knowledge

Real-Life Needs

Broke by Week 3Your meal plan money disappears. Where did it go?
Part-Time Pay VanishesYou worked 15 hours. The money lasted 4 days.
“Low-Interest” Campus LoansThe threshold is low. The cliff behind it is steep.
Scams Target StudentsFake jobs, refund scams, and “guaranteed returns” are everywhere.

Long-Term Value

  • Credit history starts now. Your first credit card affects future apartment rentals, car loans, even some of jobs.
  • Compound interest isn't just for rich people. Saving $50/month in your age of 20 can grow to six figures by retirement.
  • Understand your first paycheck. Social Security, Medicare, and 401(k) deductions won't be a mystery.

2. Basic Money Concepts

Income vs. Expenses

  • Active income: Part-time jobs, internships, tutoring, work-study.
  • Passive income: Interest from savings, dividends (rare for students, but possible).
  • Fixed expenses: Rent/dorm fees, phone bill, transit pass.
  • Flexible expenses: Eating out, streaming subscriptions, weekend plans.

Savings

  • Emergency fund:
    • 3–6 months of essential expenses
    • start with $500 (Even $100 is a start).
  • Pay yourself first: When your paycheck or financial aid lands, move 10–20% to savings immediately.

Interest: Simple vs. Compound

  • Simple interest: Calculated only on the principal.
  • Compound interest:
    • Interest on interest.
    • Example: $1,000 at 5% annual return grows to $1,629 in 10 years, $2,653 in 20 years, $4,322 in 30 years.
  • APR (Annual Percentage Rate):
    • The real yearly cost of borrowing.
    • A “payday loan” with a 15% fee for two weeks has an APR near 400%. (That's not a typo).

Inflation

  • Prices rise over time.
  • Money sitting in a checking account loses purchasing power.
    • That's why saving alone isn't enough.

3. Personal Finance Management (The Core)

Track Every Dollar

  • Tools: Apps like Mint, YNAB, or a simple spreadsheet.
  • Categories: Food, transportation, textbooks, entertainment, social, subscriptions.
  • Rule: You can't fix what you don't measure,so you need to track for 30 days before making any changes.

The 50/30/20 Rule (Adapted for Students)

CategoryPercentageWhat It Covers
Needs50%Rent/dorm, groceries, required textbooks, transportation, minimum debt payments
Wants30%Eating out, streaming, concerts, clothes, gaming, weekend trips
Savings/Debt20%Emergency fund, extra credit card payments, investing (if you have no high-interest debt)

Note: If your income is tight, aim for Needs ≤ 60% first. Adjust as your income grows.

Envelope Method (Digital Version)

  • Set a weekly spending limit for “fun money.”
  • Move that amount to a separate account or prepaid card.
  • When it's gone, it's gone. No dipping into savings.

Spending Mindset

  • Need vs. Want: Before buying, ask: “Will I die or lose my job without this?”
  • Installment traps: “0% APR for 6 months” often becomes 29.99% retroactively if you miss one payment. (Read the fine print).

4. Saving & Investing Basics

⚠️ Rule #1: No emergency fund = no investing.
  • Build 3 months of expenses in a savings account first.

⚠️ Rule #2: Only invest money you can afford to lose.
  • Your tuition and rent money is not “spare money.”

The Ladder: Low Risk → High Risk

LevelToolBest ForRisk
1High-Yield Savings Account (HYSA)Emergency fund, money needed within 1 yearVery Low
2CDs (Certificates of Deposit) / Treasury BondsMoney you won't touch for 1–3 yearsLow
3Index Funds (S&P 500, Total Market)Long-term (5+ years), monthly small contributionsMedium
4Individual StocksNot recommended for most studentsHigh

Core Principles

  • Risk and return are linked. If someone promises high returns with no risk, it's a scam.
  • Diversify. Don't put all your money in one stock, one crypto, or one “opportunity.”
  • Time in the market beats timing the market. Index fund investing is boring.

5. Credit & Borrowing

Credit Cards

  • Pay in full every month. If you carry a balance, you're paying 20%+ interest on that late-night pizza.
  • Keep utilization below 30%. If your limit is $1,000, try to keep your balance under $300.
  • Never use one card to pay another. That's a debt spiral, not a strategy.

Credit Reports & Scores

  • Your credit score (300–850) predicts how likely you are to repay debt.
  • Factors: Payment history (35%), amounts owed (30%), length of history (15%), new credit (10%), credit mix (10%).
  • One late payment can drop your score by 50–100 points. That affects apartment applications and loan rates.
🚨 Campus Loans / Predatory Lending

If a lender says “no credit check, fast cash, just your student ID,” run. Common traps:
  • Origination fees deducted upfront (you borrow $1,000, receive $850, owe $1,000)
  • Triple-digit APRs
  • Aggressive collection tactics (calling your parents, showing up at your dorm)
If you're in trouble: talk to your school's financial aid office or family, do not take another loan to pay the first one.

6. Scam Prevention

Common Scams Targeting Students

Fake Job / Task Scams“Get paid to like videos.” First task pays $5, then they ask you to deposit a check or pay for “training.”
Refund / Customer Service Scams“We overcharged you, click this link to get your refund.” The link steals your login or installs malware.
“Guaranteed” Investment PlatformsCrypto or forex platforms showing fake profits. Your deposit vanishes when you try to withdraw.
Romance / “Pig Butchering” ScamsAn online “partner” teaches you to invest on a fake platform. You “profit” on screen but can never withdraw.

The Rules (Memorize These)

  • Never pay to get a job. Legitimate employers don't ask you to pay for training or equipment upfront.
  • Never share verification codes. No real company will ever ask for the code sent to your phone.
  • If it sounds too good to be true, it is. Guaranteed 10% monthly returns don't exist.
  • Verify before you trust. Call the company's official number (not the one in the email).
If you think you've been scammed: Contact your bank immediately, file a report at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. and tell your school's financial aid office if student accounts were involved.

7. Insurance Basics

Student Health Insurance (Must-Know)

  • Most US colleges require health insurance. (You can stay on a parent's plan until age 26)
  • If you're on your school's plan, learn what's covered: copays, deductibles, out-of-network costs.
  • Never skip this. One ER visit without insurance can cost $10,000+.

Renters Insurance (If You Live Off-Campus)

  • Typically $10–20/month. It covers stolen laptops, bikes, and liability if someone gets hurt in your apartment.
  • Your dorm may not cover your belongings, remember to check before assuming.

Commercial Insurance

  • Skip life insurance and critical illness policies for now, focus on health and renters.
  • Protection first, Investing second.

Medicare (1.45%)Healthcare for seniorsRequired deduction
State Income TaxVaries by stateSome states have none (e.g., Texas, Florida)

401(k) and Employer Match

  • If your employer offers a 401(k) match, contribute at least enough to get the full match. (It's free money)
  • Example: Employer matches 50% of contributions up to 6%, you contribute 6%,and then they add 3%. That's an instant 50% return.

Build Financial Literacy

  • Read: “The Simple Path to Wealth” by JL Collins, “I Will Teach You to Be Rich” by Ramit Sethi.
  • Practice: Track expenses, automate savings, simulate index fund investing with a small amount.
  • Review: Spend 10 minutes a month reviewing your spending, that's more effective than reading 100 finance articles.

9. Your Action Checklist

Try to start with these five things this week.

  1. Download a budgeting app (Mint, YNAB, or a Google Sheet) and log every expense for 7 days.
  2. Open a high-yield savings account and move $50 into it today.
  3. Set up a $25 auto-transfer every payday.
  4. Calculate your emergency fund target: Monthly essential expenses × 3. (Write the number somewhere you'll see it)
  5. Check your subscriptions. Cancel at least one you don't use, that's $10–15/month back in your pocket.
  6. Bookmark ReportFraud.ftc.gov and save your bank's fraud hotline in your phone contacts.
